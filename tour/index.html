An fhadhb · The problem

Toxic plankton can close shellfish farms at short notice

  • Dinophysis is a microscopic alga. Mussels and other shellfish filter it from the water and build up its toxins, which cause diarrhetic shellfish poisoning (DSP).
  • To protect consumers, harvesting areas are closed when toxins exceed the legal limit. For growers, closures are costly and often come with little warning.
  • A count of 100 cells per litre is the level the Marine Institute treats as a trigger.

Can we say, a week or two ahead, where that level is likely to be reached?

How to read it

One dot per monitoring site

The colour is the chance that Dinophysis reaches 100 cells/L there in the next two weeks. Bigger dots mean a higher chance.

  • Low under 5%
  • Watch 5–15%
  • Elevated 15–30%
  • High 30% or more
  • Paler, dashed no sample yet this week

Torthaí · How good is it?

Modestly better than the usual pattern, and we can say how much

0.312 vs 0.231nationally, 2022–26
0.338 vs 0.203South-West (Kerry / West Cork)
~0.04what random guessing would score

In plain words: the score (PR-AUC, 0 to 1) measures how well the forecast puts the real bloom weeks at the top of its list. The comparison is “what normally happens at this site in this week of the year”. Blooms are rare (about 4% of site-weeks), so both numbers are far above chance. The point is the gap.

Caveat: for the same number of alarms it does not catch more closures than the Marine Institute’s own simple rule (41 vs 46 of 62 South-West closures), but it raises far fewer false-alarm runs (41 vs 124–130). It is a surveillance aid, not a safety system.

Tástálacha · The test log

122 tests run, 62 null results reported

  • Every experiment is pre-registered where possible and logged with its verdict; all results are reported, including null results.
  • Recent counts are the forecast. What was in the water at and near a site in recent weeks carries almost all the skill; a simple logistic model gets within about 0.01.
  • Environmental extras did not help: sea temperature, heatwaves, wind, chlorophyll, currents and rivers added nothing once recent counts were known.

Tonnta teasa mara · Marine heatwaves

More heatwaves, but no detectable effect on blooms

+29 days / decadeheatwave days per station, 2005–25 (suggestive)
15ecology tests: no detectable effect
1.39 → 1.00the “40% more blooms” claim, once compared fairly
  • Heatwaves happen in summer, and so do blooms. Compare the same site in the same month, and the apparent “+40%” disappears.
  • June 2023 brought a severe shelf heatwave, yet national Dinophysis counts and closures were below normal. A heatwave is not a bloom.
  • Inside sheltered shellfish bays, temperature loggers record a median 82% more heatwave days than satellites see.

Tocsainí eile · Other toxins & species

One promising lead, and several clear “no”s

  • Amnesic toxin (ASP) in king scallops: a toxin-history forecast scored 0.748 vs 0.443 for “same as last time” in Ireland. Promising, but it did not replicate in France.
  • “Hidden bloom” flag: a toxin rise with no preceding high count. Flagged sites had a 23% chance of a ≥100 count within 21 days vs 5.1% otherwise, and it replicates in France.
  • Paralytic (PSP), azaspiracid (AZP) toxins and Karenia mikimotoi: no gain over simple baselines.

Samplaí · Sampling & turnaround

When you sample matters more than how fast the lab is

Share of samples public before the Sunday forecast

Mon90%
Tue75%
Wed40%
Thu2%
  • Even with zero lab delay, skill would rise only from 0.312 to 0.324.
  • Knowing this week’s counts is the biggest lever we found (up to +0.063). The missing information sits in late-week samples.
  • Ideas (untested hypotheses): sample earlier in the week, issue on Tuesday, sample twice weekly at South-West sentinel sites in peak season, and add fast on-site screening.
